# Engineering Management Ladder

*(References are embedded throughout)*

## Overview

The performance characteristics described can be useful in conversations with your manager around quarterly goal setting.
The document is not meant to be used as a checklist for promotion. Evaluate levels and their performance characteristics holistically.

Use this for development conversations and goal setting. Evaluate scope, judgment, behavior, and sustained outcomes together.  Agree on opportunities and evidence with the manager; use the employer's actual promotion process. [GitLab career development](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/careers/management/management-career-development/)

The progression moves from guiding a team, through coordinating several teams toward strategic goals, to defining organizational direction and investment. Tactical, operational, and strategic work overlap at every level; the balance changes as responsibility grows. [VP, Director, what?](https://www.theengineeringmanager.com/managing-managers/vp-director-what/)

- **Accountability carries forward; daily tasks change.** A Director remains accountable for feedback and delivery, while managers conduct most individual coaching and team planning. Assess whether the system works through those leaders.
- **Scope includes complexity and consequences.** Consider ambiguity, dependencies, decision authority, customer impact, financial responsibility, and the number of leadership layers. Headcount and geography provide context.
- **Sustainable results remain essential.** Preserve long-term growth, retention, inclusion, psychological safety, and customer trust. Shipping and maintaining a capable, healthy team are joint responsibilities. [ELC startup VPE discussion](https://sfelc.com/podcasts/how-to-become-a-startup-vpe-martin-casado-sonal-chokshi)
- **Management is a distinct profession.** Practice deliberately, learn from mistakes, seek feedback, and build a peer group. Develop an interest in achieving impact through and with others. [Supplied Will Larson transcript, 1:25–3:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TkA5A7BJF2k&t=85s)
- **Titles are org dependent.** M3 through M8 are just labels. Compare actual responsibility across companies; a startup VP and a large-company Director may have overlap.


## Levels

| Local level | Title | Accountability | Typical scope and authority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M3 | Engineering Manager | Team performance, customer results, and operational, people, and roadmap ownership. | One area or a few smaller areas; commonly one or two teams of ICs. Stays close to day-to-day operation and customer needs. Sets performance objectives, conducts reviews, and recommends compensation. Management as more than half of working time, often guided/supported by more senior managers. |
| M4 | Senior Engineering Manager | Budget, performance, and results for a complex area, a medium team, or several small teams. | Multiple substantial workstreams, significant ambiguity, and wider organizational influence. Expands the area's operating capability as complexity grows. Sets expectations for people, operations, and roadmaps; coaches peer or newer managers and improves manager-team decisions. May own cost centers or multiple sites. Exercises delegated authority for hiring, performance, pay, and exits. |
| M5 | Director of Engineering | Responsibility for a strategic company objective and the organization delivering it. Accountable for both department outcomes and strategic direction, delivered through managers and senior technical leaders. | Owns a larger service area and its operation; sets department priorities with little direction. Understands its place in the company, makes relative investment decisions, and can release resources to other areas. Hires and develops managers. |
| M6 | Senior Director of Engineering | A major business area or complex portfolio of strategic objectives, delivered through and in collaboration with other leaders. | Often a large product line, regional function, or global organization reporting to a VP or SVP. Develops leaders, improves quality beyond the area, and drives changes affecting throughput, retention, and customer outcomes. Has major financial oversight or influence on operating expenses. |
| M7 | Vice President of Engineering | Engineering's contribution to company success and the performance of the engineering organization. | Owns engineering operating expenses and executive alignment. Builds a leadership organization that delivers today and develops future capability. The kernel describes all of engineering; a divisional VP elsewhere may have a narrower remit. |
| M8 | CTO | Business success through technology and, where assigned, the engineering organization. | Shapes company technology direction, major technical commitments, and long-term capability as other C-suite leadership. People-organization ownership varies; establish the split with the VP, CEO, and other executives. |

### Analogy

A memorable analogy:

- (Team or Tech Lead is the "first mate" on a ship)
- M3 captains a small crew on day trips
- M4 takes a mid-sized ship through rough water
- M5 takes a large ship on long voyages
- M6 directs a fleet through a storm
- M7 decides the number and sizes of fleets
- M8 designs ports, fleets, and ships

## Role essentials

### Where the time goes

1. **Direction and alignment:** connect company goals, customer needs, engineering capability, and investment. Give teams enough context to decide locally and give leadership an accurate view of constraints and opportunities.
2. **People and organization:** recruit, coach, give feedback, address performance, develop leaders, and design responsibilities so the organization can operate without constant personal intervention.
3. **Delivery and learning:** agree on outcomes, understand actual capacity, remove obstacles, inspect progress and system health, and revise commitments when evidence changes.

Technical credibility supports all three. Understand the relevant systems well enough to question assumptions and tradeoffs. 
*Often managers should avoid being responsible for implementation that blocks others or that is in the delivery critical path.*

### The three most important duties

1. Results
2. Retention
3. Growth

- <https://www.manager-tools.com/2015/06/results-and-retention-part-1-hall-fame-guidance>
- <https://www.manager-tools.com/2015/06/results-and-retention-part-2-hall-fame-guidance>
- <https://www.manager-tools.com/2025/11/executives-two-responsibilities-mt>

Tools:

1. **Alignment:** make goals, ownership, constraints, and tradeoffs clear. A leadership team should resolve conflicting priorities before asking teams to execute them.
2. **Effective operating practices:** improve or remove ineffective process; add it where it helps decisions, coordination, quality, and learning. Assign clear owners for system access, risk, and applicable compliance commitments.
3. **Balancing needs:** partner with customers, Product, Design, Engineering, Finance, Security, and other functions. Agree on an intake and prioritization path appropriate to the role's scope.

### Skills

Understand engineering feasibility and risk; communicate clearly; build relationships and trust; make timely **decisions**; develop **people**; and connect delivery to business **value**. Technical specialists, managers, and product leaders contribute different expertise. Decision ownership should be explicit, and disagreement should be possible even when an executive is in the room.

### Measures of success

Use a small set of measures that support a named decision. Record the baseline, intended outcome, responsible leader, review cadence, and important limitations.

| Measure | What it helps decide | Illustrative evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer and business outcomes | Whether the work is worth continuing or changing. | Adoption, customer success, retention, revenue contribution, or cost-to-serve appropriate to the product. Engineering and Product share responsibility for the outcome. |
| Delivery and service commitments | Whether commitments are credible and services meet customer needs. | Forecast changes and their causes, delivered scope, reliability against agreed commitments, and response to material incidents. |
| Engineering improvement | Where engineering should improve its own operating practices. | Review delays, build time, flaky tests, avoidable toil, and developer feedback. Explain their relationship to delivery or risk when seeking investment. |
| Organizational health and capacity | Whether results can be sustained. | Engagement, retention, growth opportunities, performance clarity, leadership coverage, and workload. Interpret signals in context. |
| Investment | Whether people, systems, and vendors are assigned to the highest-value opportunities. | Spending against plan, expected and realized benefit, displaced work, and reviewed alternatives. |

The distinction between business-facing planning measures and internal engineering improvement measures comes from the [supplied Will Larson transcript, 12:36–17:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TkA5A7BJF2k&t=756s). Roadmap completion and improving KPIs are useful evidence alongside customer value and organizational health.

### Director, VP, and CTO responsibilities

The Director coordinates an operational area and develops the managers delivering it. The VP keeps engineering aligned with the company, plans its evolution, and protects focus while ensuring effective operation through Directors. [Director overview](https://leaddev.com/hiring/what-director-engineering-glue-keeping-engineering-together), [VP account](https://leaddev.com/career-development/reality-being-vp-engineering)

Document the VP/CTO agreement: people organization, delivery commitments, technical strategy, architecture decision ownership, product-strategy input, budget, and external representation. Startup roles may combine several responsibilities; assign explicit owners as the company grows. The CTO may be a technical strategist, engineering leader, researcher, or external representative depending on the company. The agreement is a synthesis of the [ELC discussion](https://sfelc.com/podcasts/how-to-become-a-startup-vpe-martin-casado-sonal-chokshi), which emphasizes the variability of that title.

### First 90 days in a senior role — synthesis

Diagnose the business, systems, organization, and important relationships while addressing a small number of urgent problems. Validate what stakeholder complaints actually indicate, agree on the remit and priorities, and choose improvements that demonstrate value without assuming the entire organization should immediately adopt a previous employer's practices. This applies the [supplied Larson transcript, 6:48–11:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TkA5A7BJF2k&t=408s); it is an onboarding practice rather than a level definition.
